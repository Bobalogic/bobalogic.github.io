Trinca e Molha, 2025 to now

We planned for 40 burgers a day. We sold 170 on the first.

How our smash burger stand at the Festas de Oeiras paid back its investment in four days, and what the sales data taught me about where the money comes from.

Where it started

On a trip to Budapest in 2023 I discovered smash burgers. The food at the Festas de Oeiras, our town's summer festival, was always the same: bifanas, entremeadas and sardines. I wanted to offer something different.

In 2025 I started Trinca e Molha with two friends, Hugo Veiga and Marta Pereira, who designed the brand. 170 vendors applied for 13 food spots at the festival. We sent our application on the last possible day and got one.

Before the first burger

A festival stand needs more than a good recipe. We worked through the HACCP food-safety requirements and planned the electrical setup, including a three-phase 16A connection and a separate generator so the griddle could run on its own supply.

The menu was deliberately short: three smash burgers at €8.50 to €9.50, snails, beer at €1.50 with a happy hour from 16:00 to 18:30, and cider.

The hardest part was predicting sales. With no data to go on, we kept our start-up costs as low as possible, so that if it went badly the loss would be small. We planned the downside before the upside.

Opening week

We had planned for about 40 burgers a day. On the first day we sold more than 170 and ran out of stock. On the second day, at 20:30, we had to call our parents to bring more meat. By the end of the fourth day we had recovered our initial investment. Later that week we closed early again, at 21:30, because the meat ran out.

From the third day on, the fix was simple: we bought much more stock.

The burger named after the mayor

One of our three burgers was the Isaltino, named after Isaltino Morais, the Mayor of Oeiras. It came with bacon and a spicy sauce, because he divides opinion. The mayor came to the stand and filmed one of his videos trying it. After that, demand exploded.

What the sales data showed

Across the run we sold about 5,720 items. Burgers were only a third of them, but they brought in about 69% of the revenue. That follows from the price gap: a burger sold for around €9, a beer for €1.50. Drinks and snacks moved the volume; the burger carried the business.

Year two: what we changed

In 2026 we went back to the Festas de Oeiras, and our opening was about 30% stronger than the year before. We used what we had learned:

  • Pricing. Each burger went up €0.50, to €9 to €10, and fries are now included with every one.
  • A new product. The Marquês, with a herb mayonnaise and marinated cucumber, sold about 150 units in its first days.
  • A partnership. Don Pavili, a well-known local ice cream shop, runs a dessert stand next to ours.
  • Filling the quiet hours. On weekend afternoons we host a World Cup sticker swap at the tables in front of the stand.
  • A new audience. Selling cider brought in noticeably more young customers.

Today the brand serves around 6,000 customers a year through the festival, pop-ups and private events. The longer-term plan is a physical location.

Getting noticed

Our social posts reached more than 300,000 people, and New in Oeiras by NIT covered us in both years. IPAM, a leading Portuguese marketing school, invited me to present our sales analysis in a guest talk and to launch a live student challenge on consumer insight, product innovation and growth.
